The Brewer has been in Munich since before anyone thought to write it down. He claims to have arrived with the first brewing ordinance, which amuses him — he was already working before anyone thought to legislate what he was doing.

His brewery occupies a low building near the river, and the smell of grain and warm copper travels further than it should. He produces mostly beer, as Munich requires, but also gin — precise, clean, made in small batches that circulate among those who know to ask. The gin is not for sale. It is for specific evenings.

He joined the Collective the way most people join things: gradually, then suddenly. He had been supplying the Gardener's gatherings for years before the question of membership arose. By the time it was asked formally, it was already a formality.

The Brewer does not moralise about celebration. He understands that joy is not a luxury but a form of maintenance — that a city which does not know how to rest will eventually stop knowing how to work. He provides the conditions for joy and then steps back.

His long nights are not sad ones. They are the nights when the work is done and the gin is poured and someone says something that could not have been said before the glass was full. He values these nights. He has collected a great many of them.
