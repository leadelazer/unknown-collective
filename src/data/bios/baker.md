The Baker arrived in the city in 1690, during a winter that tested its patience. He came not as a merchant but as a craftsman — a man who woke before dawn and kept his own hours, indifferent to the tempo of the city around him.

He had learned the old way. Slow fermentation. Stone-ground flour. A sourness in the dough that came not from shortcuts but from time itself. The city's mills ground faster and finer; his did not. People came anyway.

His induction into the Collective was something of an accident. The Curator had been following the scent of his bread for weeks before they finally spoke. The Baker said three things in that first conversation — quiet, precise, and slightly upside-down — and the Curator recognised the kind of mind the Collective had been missing.

He does not argue or insist. He asks the questions others are too practical to ask: what would remain if this were taken away? What does patience cost the person who no longer has it? While the Mapmaker charts what exists, the Baker wonders what would be left if it were gone.

He keeps a sourdough starter that he claims predates the Collective itself. Nobody has verified this. Nobody has disproved it either.
