Amelia was born in a small village in Ireland, the air heavy with the scent of fresh earth and wildflowers. The sound of raindrops tapping against windowpanes was a constant backdrop, marking the rhythm of her childhood. Her father tended to a nursery, cultivating a variety of plants. Her mother painted, depicting the colors of their surroundings in vivid splashes across canvas.

At eighteen, Amelia’s life changed. Duchess Luise of Bavaria saw her work during a visit to Ireland. The duchess invited Amelia to Munich, offering her a position in the estate gardens. Here, among formal hedges and ornate fountains, Amelia blossomed. She mixed blooms into displays, a blend of joy and urgency. Her arrangements carried a newness that was both bold and unrefined. She embraced spontaneity, designing without restraint.

Her relationship with the Curator grew in unexpected ways. He walked the estate's gardens, critiquing Amelia’s installations with a cold gaze. They argued about balance—his view fixed on tradition, hers on the potential of fresh blooms. These discussions led to a cautious respect. In the archives of the estate, he would pull out rare volumes of floral art, sharing insights. Amelia listened, taking notes, her enthusiasm unflagging.

Now, her days revolve around sourcing flowers from local markets and organizing them for various events. She opens her shop each morning in the neighborhood of Altstadt-Lehel, her hands working swiftly to create bouquets tailored to each client's wishes. The shop has a rustic charm, with vases lining the walls and a counter cluttered with tools. Each arrangement carries a message, a coded language of blooms she interprets with care.

There is a nagging discomfort. While her floristry thrives, her trust in spontaneity often leads to misunderstandings. Customers sometimes misinterpret the meanings, leaving her to ponder their reactions. The joy of creation collides with the reality of miscommunication, leaving Amelia to question the price of her innocent enthusiasm.
