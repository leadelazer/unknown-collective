The Cook arrived in Munich in 1702. He opened no shop, posted no sign, and made no announcement — he simply began to cook, in a small kitchen off the Viktualienmarkt, for whoever arrived. No one could later recall who had told them to go there.

People began to notice: conversations lasted longer in his presence. Feuds dissolved without resolution — simply faded, somewhere between the soup and the bread. His meals stretched past their natural conclusion, as if time itself had grown a larger appetite.

The Baker noticed it too: on the days when the Cook passed his shop, the dough rose faster — not forced, simply encouraged toward readiness. The Baker learned to time his schedule around these moments, though neither of them ever spoke of it directly.

The Duchess first brought it to the Collective's attention in 1703, during a particularly tense meeting. As voices rose around the table, the Cook placed his hands flat on the wood surface. The anger didn't disappear — it transformed into something more digestible. After that, he was always invited.

He rarely speaks. Children follow him through the market regardless; vendors find their produce staying fresh longer in his wake. He offers no explanation for any of it. He simply cooks, steadily and without ceremony, and trusts that what is needed will find its way to the table.
