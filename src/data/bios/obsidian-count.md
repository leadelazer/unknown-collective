Leopold von Hohenberg was born in 1820 into an ancient line of Hamburg nobility. He was always the family's black sheep — rejecting the trappings of his aristocratic life in favour of law and philosophy. At eighteen, he left for Heidelberg, a path few of his stature would take.

In the 1840s, dispatched on diplomatic missions to negotiate trading rights in Africa and Asia, Leopold was exposed to the harsh realities of colonial exploitation. The inequities he witnessed disturbed him deeply — left marks that did not fade with travel.

He clung to law as his sanctuary, his shield against the grey areas he had been forced to confront. His reputation for unwavering fairness spread across borders, eventually reaching the ears of the Munich Collective, which was struggling to impose order on the chaos unleashed by the Bind.

In 1875, Leopold moved to Munich. His interventions introduced a semblance of balance to the Collective — a framework of principle where there had been none. For this, he earned the title Obsidian Count, and with it both the respect and the wariness of his peers.

He remains the Collective's strictest conscience. He is not beloved — he is trusted, which is different, and in his estimation, more durable. He has seen the worst of human nature across three continents and chose to oppose it with principle rather than power.
