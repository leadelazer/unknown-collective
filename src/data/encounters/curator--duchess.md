---
title: The Induction — Autumn in the Collective Hall, 1720
---

The vote was held in the third-floor room with the low ceiling and the crooked shutter. Seven members were present. The Duchess had counted them before she arrived: she knew which two would abstain, which one would speak too long, and which would watch her face instead of the candidate. What she had not counted on was how long Freddy would stand without speaking when they finally let him in.

He stood at the far end of the table, coat damp from the street, hands at his sides. He had been a baker's son and then a soldier and then a scholar of law, and the old guard looked at him the way they looked at a street map they couldn't quite reconcile with their memory of the city. One of them — she would not record his name in any account — called him an instrument of the Duchess's sentiment. The word landed like a coin on stone.

She did not answer it. She passed three folded pages across the table instead: the argument for his induction written in the language of the archive, impersonal and precise. She had been preparing it for eleven months. The document cited no personal connection. It cited only the nature of the Bind, and the kind of mind required to map what it was doing to the city's records.

He was voted in by five to two. The Duchess closed her portfolio and said nothing further. Later that evening, in the corridor, Freddy asked her why she had not spoken in his defence. She looked at him for a moment. *The document was the defence,* she said. *A spoken argument they could dismiss as feeling. A written one they have to file.*
